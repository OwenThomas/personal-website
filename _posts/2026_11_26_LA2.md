
Turning to fiction that approaches similar scientific territory to *Arcadia*, we have a surprising number of options. The shortest possible stylistic distance to travel would naturally be to other Tom Stoppard plays: none of them pull of the explicit combination of science and humanities with quite the same éclat as *Arcadia*, but unsurprisingly a similar voice emerges in other works. His 1966 breakthrough hit play *Rosencrantz and Guildenstern Are Dead* toys with logical and probabilistic paradoxes, further expanding to physics in the 1990 film adaptation, which Stoppard also directed. Likewise, his final play *Leopoldstadt* (2020) reflecting on his Viennese Jewish ancestors is seasoned with references to Carl Friedrich Gauss's childhood feat of quickly summing the integers from 1 to 100, despite his teacher's intention to shut him up for a while.

</figure>

*Hapgood* (1988) is another play of Stoppard's from a mere 5 years before *Arcadia*, and sets itself a similar task of approaching scientific themes head-on. This time, he draws on quantum mechanics and its famed uncertainty principle, provided with an allegorical home within a cold-war espionage thriller. The analogy between the uncertainty encountered by the spymaster title character and the quantum uncertainty is less exact than those pursued in *Arcadia*. In principle objects in the sub-microscopic quantum mechanical world can never be fully resolved in all of familiar physical quantities of interest simultaneously: entities will be particles or waves, or possess well-defined location or momentum, depending on how the experiment is designed. By contrast, *Hapgood's* Cold war intrigues involve classical systems that are in principle fully identifiable, but with profound information asymmetry driven by political and personal concerns. The unifying factor between the two is that the act of measurement changes the system itself: a quantum system resolves itself into a specific configuration defined by the mode of measurement of the investigator, while seasoned espionage professionals will change their forms and allegiances when the questions become too probing.

The scientific information is presented a little more disjointedly compared to *Arcadia*, with the relevant information coming as interludes and asides from characters who just so happen to have an amateur interest in the material:

> Nobody knows. Somehow light is continuous and also discontinuous. The experimenter makes the choice. You get what you interrogate for. And you want to know if I’m a wave or a particle. Every month at the pool, I and my friend Georgi exchange material. When the experiment is over, you have a result. I am your joe. But they also have a result: because you have put in my briefcase enough information to keep me credible as a Russian sleeper activated by my KGB control; which is what Georgi thinks he is. So naturally he gives me enough information to keep me credible as a British joe. Frankly, I can’t remember which side I’m supposed to be working for, and it is not in fact necessary for me to know.

There is an intriguing anti-Bernard character who is also cynical about the value of science, but instead thinks that the applications to warfare are overstated: in the military context of *Hapgood* this is felt to be a bad thing, but given their responsibility for the atomic weaponry driving the cold war, physicists may find this a refreshingly cynical approach to their legacy.

</figure>

There are other mathematical references in *Hapgood*, which are interesting but not key to the underlying analogy of quantum and espionage uncertainty. The Seven Bridges of Königsberg puzzle gets an airing via Immanuel Kant and Leonhard Euler, and the concept of prime numbers is invoked as a lead-in for a "prime suspect" joke. The play is considered among Stoppard's few missteps and is rarely revived: it seems that he needed a second iteration at the challenge of writing scientifically-literate theatre that he would finally nail in *Arcadia*, and we should be grateful he showed persistence to the task.

Fortunately, there is another playwright who wrote a successful play on the theme of quantum mechanics: Michael Frayn and his 1998 play *Copenhagen*. Frayn, also known for his incredibly funny backstage-farce-within-a-farce *Noises Off*, took the subject of the 1941 meeting between the German Werner Heisenberg, the Dane Niels Bohr, and his wife Margrethe. The two former are winners of the Nobel in Physics trying to preserve their academic relationship under the pressure of the ongoing occupation of Denmark by Nazi Germany. We do not have much surviving information concerning the contents of their conversation, which Frayn turns to his thematic advantage again by analogy with  Heisenberg's uncertainty principle: the audience is presented with several different versions of how the conversation might have played out, each terminated with a call of "Another Draft!" when something rings hollow to one of the characters. We do not know which iteration is entirely accurate: we cannot, just as we cannot know all the details of the quantum mechanical objects that these scientists grappled with throughout their careers.

<figure>
  <img src="frayn-copenhagen.jpg">
  <figcaption>Michael Frayn's Copenhagen.</figcaption>
</figure>

The play itself is not comedic, with the intensity of the historical setting becoming manifest in their relationship: the former teacher-and-student have become rivals both academically and politically. Heisenberg had an uneasily relaxed relationship with National Socialism, and the Jewish Bohr calls him out eloquently for his arrogance towards his smaller host nation:

> My dear Heisenberg, it would be an easy mistake to make, to think that the citizens of a small nation, of a small nation overrun, wantonly and cruelly overrun, by its more powerful neighbour, don’t have exactly the same feelings of national pride as their conquerors, exactly the same love of their country.

Margrethe Bohr plays an active and crucial role in their conversation, holding her own position on the conflicts discussed, and helping de-escalate the friction between the substantial egos of the two men. There is a 2002 BBC film adaptation which is worth watching, although it cuts a substantial amount of material from the play and reduces the staging to an intimate country house. The dialogue has an extra tautness, and the low staging budget escalates the attention on the relationships presented: 

> I’m a danger to man kind; I’m also your guest. I’m a particle; I’m also a wave

Looking more broadly outside of the realm of theatre for scientifically-themed literature, there is a wealth of choices to be enjoyed. One work, contemporary to *Arcadia*, also rode the waves of on-trend Chaos theory to mainstream popularity: Michael Crichton's *Jurassic Park* (1990). 

<figure>
  <img src="dragon-curves.png">
  <figcaption>The fractal Dragon Curves iterating throughout Jurassic Park.</figcaption>
</figure>

The subject is first approached through the incrementally nested dragon curves present at the start of each section, with the increasingly dense reptilian figures teasing the explicit references to chaos theory coming later. The field takes human form as mathematician Ian Malcolm, who receives a thorough simultaneous dressing-up and dressing-down in his introduction to the reader:

> Ian Malcolm was one of the most famous of the new generation of mathematicians who were openly interested in “how the real world works.” These scholars broke with the cloistered tradition of mathematics in several important ways. For one thing, they used computers constantly, a practice traditional mathematicians frowned on. For another, they worked almost exclusively with nonlinear equations, in the emerging field called chaos theory. For a third, they appeared to care that their mathematics described something that actually existed in the real world. And finally, as if to emphasize their emergence from academia into the world, they dressed and spoke with what one senior mathematician called “a deplorable excess of personality.” In fact, they often behaved like rock stars.

Parts of the book are dedicated to explaining the basic ideas of chaos theory, with other characters conveniently taking the role of confused Socratic interlocutor to help the exposition to flow as clearly as possible. Like *Arcadia*, the scientific material covered is in large part drawn from Gleick's book, as Crichton acknowledges in the afterword: this is no bad thing, as the general public will probably digest the material more easily when encountered between the high-octane events of a technothriller with resuscitated dinosaurs. The then-trendiness of chaos theory is also mentioned, possibly represented in another character's more cynical take:

> Chaos theory describes nonlinear systems. It's now become a very broad theory that's been used to study everything from the stock market, to rioting crowds, to brain waves during epilepsy. A very fashionable theory. Very trendy to apply it to any complex system where there might be unpredictability.

The crudeness of the narrative logic is thus partially acknowledged: "experimental dinosaur theme parks are inherently unpredictable" plus "chaos theory is a new a field of science with a cool name designed to model the unpredictable" equals "chaos theory is central to the plot of this book". It's not fully logical, and it's not fully illogical: it's *Jurassic Park*. There is apparently a film version which seems to have done [pretty well](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_highest-grossing_films#Timeline_of_highest-grossing_films).

It would be interesting to find scientifically-inclined works more firmly rooted in the classical literary tradition that *Arcadia* itself blossoms from. There is a frustratingly awkward relationship between "highbrow" literature and science fiction, and surprisingly few writers are well-respected as insiders by both communities[^1]. However, two writers who have historically straddled both worlds with some success and touched on similar thermodynamic themes to are Kurt Vonnegut and Thomas Pynchon.

Kurt Vonnegut originally trained as a biochemist at Cornell following family pressure but eventually emerged as an oddly scientifically literate writer of fiction. His 1963 novel *Cat's Cradle* is a bleakly humorous cold war satire set on a small Caribbean island: the narrative is dominated by the looming presence of ice-9, the most prominent reference to thermodynamics of all his books. Ice-9 is a newly developed alternative state of matter for H<sub>2</sub>O molecules that irreversibly convert any other forms of water it contacts into itself. This property makes it an existential threat to the planet, as if it were released into the wild then any open bodies of water of our ecosystem would be permanently frozen solid. The characters are well aware of this, and one of them carries a sample round in a thermos flask as a type of apocalyptic bargaining chip: the novel appearing the year after the Cuban missile crisis makes clear the subtext concerning the real and present threat of nuclear weapons. The concept of irreversibility comes up in *Arcadia* in various guises, with some decisions being harder to undo than others, and consequences being permanently lost to the entropic forces of time.

  <img src="vonnegut-cats-cradle.jpg">
  <figcaption>Kurt Vonnegut's Cat's Cradle.</figcaption>
</figure>

Thomas Pynchon also studied science at Cornell, initially choosing engineering physics with similarly little enthusiasm to Vonnegut. Like Vonnegut, Pynchon went on to serve in the military but then returned to Cornell to complete a B.A. in in English. However, his scientific education reappears across his literary oeuvre, most explicitly in his 1966 novel *The Crying of Lot 49*. The novel is a short, dense, postmodern mystery about postal systems, full of disparate allusions across the arts and sciences. Entropy, a measure of disorder of a system, is a key theme, and the relationship between the physical and informatic definitions of the term is laid out: 

> She did gather that there were two distinct kinds of this entropy. One having to do with heat-engines, the other to do with communication. The equation for one, back in the '30's, had looked very like the equation for the other. It was a coincidence. The two fields were entirely unconnected, except at one point: Maxwell's Demon. As the Demon sat and sorted his molecules into hot and cold, the system was said to lose entropy. But somehow the loss was offset by the information the Demon gained about what molecules were where.

The name for the informatic meaning of entropy was famously suggested to its inventor Claude Shannon by John von Neumann, partly to clarify and partly to obfuscate things:

> You should call it entropy, for two reasons. In the first place your uncertainty function has been used in statistical mechanics under that name, so it already has a name. In the second place, and more important, no one really knows what entropy really is, so in a debate you will always have the advantage.

<figure>
  <img src="pynchon-crying.jpg">
  <figcaption>Thomas Pynchon's The Crying of Lot 49.</figcaption>
</figure>
Spanning the two definitions is Maxwell's Demon, a hypothetical being who can seemingly violate the Second Law of Thermodynamics by increasing the order of a physical system for zero physical energy. This is explained in a dialogue between two characters in the novel:

> "The Demon could sit in a box among air molecules that were moving at all different random speeds, and sort out the fast molecules from the slow ones. Fast molecules have more energy than slow ones. Concentrate enough of them in one place and you have a region of high temperature. You can then use the difference in temperature between this hot region of the box and any cooler region, to drive a heat engine. Since the Demon only sat and sorted, you wouldn't have put any real work into the system. So you would be violating the Second Law of Thermodynamics, getting something for nothing, causing perpetual, motion."
> "Sorting isn't work?...Tell them down at the post office, you'll find yourself in a mailbag headed for Fairbanks, Alaska, without even a fragile sticker going for you."
> "It's mental work... But not work in the thermodynamic sense."

The Demon thus acts to convert physical disorder into mental entropy. As the novel ends, the Demon acts as a metaphor for the other characters' efforts to unpick the mysterious events that they encounter. Solving the mystery does not change the physical state of the world, but it does straighten out their understanding of it, which is work enough in itself that they can take pride in.

Finally, I would like to close with reference to the real historical character that *Arcadia* references: the life and times of Ada Lovelace. [Several books](https://fivebooks.com/best-books/ada-lovelace-ursula-martin/) have been written about her incredible life, including fine biographies and representations in fiction. She features prominently in William Gibson and Bruce Sterling's *The Difference Engine*, a 1990 novel which played a seminal role in the development of steampunk. The idea of a forgotten Victorian "enchantress of numbers" manifesting her genius via whirring cogs and gearwheels is certainly substantial enough to inspire a genre of its own. She also joined the Y2K zeitgeist as an ethereal cyberspace presence in the 1997 film *Conceiving Ada*.

I would like to highlight one explicit Lovelace representation in particular drawn from that curious cousin of the theatrical tradition: the 2015 graphic novel *The Thrilling Adventures of Lovelace and Babbage* by Sydney Padua. Graphic novels and theatre show a surprising affinity: they are two media forms that are both literary and inherently visible, both grounded in the written word but experienced in their truest form as a visual experience.

The graphic novel has a fun premise: what if Babbage successfully got his computing machines beyond the theoretical stage, and Lovelace was able to execute her programs in fictional reality? The narrative is an entertaining ride accompanying the title characters through various japes: Padua has clearly done thorough research with much of the main characters' dialogue being drawn from real correspondence, giving a rich representation of the main characters' personalities: Babbage the extroverted, insecure curmudgeon with a surprising hatred for street musicians, and Lovelace the more introverted, impassioned visionary with a hidden weakness for betting on horse races. The title characters meet other luminaries of their day (apparently accurately given Babbage's fondness for hosting parties), including Queen Victoria, Samuel Taylor Coleridge and George Eliot[^2]. 
<figure>
  <img src="lovelace-engine.png">
  <figcaption>Lovelace and Babbage, alongside the finally realised Analytical Engine.</figcaption>
</figure>
The graphic novel has extensive footnotes, which are thorough and small enough to interrupt the flow of reading the panels if you are inclined to read them all. There may be two ways of reading the book: one in which you race through the plot without historical context, and another slower reading fully informed by Padua's substantial investigations on the topic. At times, the sheer density of extra information becomes too much, which the author acknowledges by literally driving a train through them at one point:
<figure>
  <img src="lovelace-train.png">
  <figcaption>One of the more creative ways I have seen for removing excess footnotes.</figcaption>
</figure>
Clearly she was showing self-awareness concerning the question ever-present in a work of this type: Are we here to inform or to entertain? Do we want to accompany our protagonists on their zany adventures, or do we want to ensure the world knows the facts of the matter? Perhaps the latter can be achieved surreptitiously by leading with the former, but the work as a whole has a tension between the two that is never fully resolved. Perhaps it doesn't need to be: the reader can bounce between the entertaining visuals and the educational extras when the lure of the narrative has them successfully hooked.

*The Thrilling Adventures of Lovelace and Babbage* emerged toward the end of the period of mainstream interest in the steampunk aesthetic: the book is perhaps less well-remembered than it might have been considering the genre is no longer as on-trend. However, in our post-*Poor Things* times then steampunk might be emerging as its own meta-retro era, even if its Victorian-techno-nostalgia probably wouldn't stand up to the interrogation of today's identity politics and anti-Big-Tech cynicism. Still, it's an engaging read, loaded with enough visual spectacle to make the historical information go down easy, like a pill thumbed into a slice of a cheese for a skeptical pet.

And yet, the public are not sick dogs, good storytelling is not a well-aged gouda, and scientific knowledge is not a bitter pill that that we must choke down to relieve our agonies. The best works that combine quantitative and qualitative wisdom do not need to compromise one for the other, as each contains fundamental and complementary insight into the human experience. *Arcadia* pursues this synthesis via the artistic medium of the theatre: as the boundaries between eras dissolve towards the end of the play, the characters start to voice their roles in our lives across time:

> Valentine   She didn’t have the maths, not remotely. She saw what things meant, way ahead, like seeing a picture.
> Septimus   This is not science. This is story-telling.

*Arcadia* is science-informed storytelling at its best: I hope this and the previous post have communicated just why, and what else you could turn to for something similar. Enjoy!
